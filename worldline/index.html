Method

WHAT THIS INTERFACE IS AND ISN'T

WORLDLINE is a working model of how climate, conflict, migration, resources, power, and space infrastructure interact over time — not a forecast. Every number on the map is computed live from assumptions you can see and change in the SCENARIO panel.

THE FOUR CATEGORIES

OBSERVEDCurrent or historical measurement.
PROJECTEDA present trend extended forward.
MODELEDAn output of this model, under stated assumptions.
SPECULATIVEA structurally possible future. Read the number as a shape, not a quantity.

HOW THE NUMBERS ARE MADE

Each region carries declared baseline parameters (governance, military posture, water stress, and so on), informed by real assessments — IPCC, UN Population Prospects, SIPRI, UNHCR, Aqueduct, and others listed under VIEW SOURCES on every signal. Those baselines are then moved by a small deterministic model as time and your assumptions change. The WHY? panel on any signal shows the actual arithmetic — the terms shown are the terms summed, nothing is hidden.

The sources are the evidence base the parameters were drawn from. They are not the origin of any specific probability shown here — that distinction matters, and the interface tries never to blur it.

UNCERTAINTY, DRAWN

Confidence falls the further a signal reaches into the future. A confident signal renders as a tight point; an uncertain one renders as a diffuse scatter with no clear center. You can see how much is known before you read a single number.

SEA LEVEL AND THE COASTLINE

There is no elevation or bathymetry data in this prototype — that would mean shipping a terrain model, not a cartographic outline. The blue band along the coast is today's land that the modelled sea-level number (shown in the corner, updating as you move the timeline) now claims, weighted by how coastally exposed the nearest declared region is. The dotted line inside it is today's real coastline, kept visible so the band reads as loss, not just a different map. Treat its shape as illustrative, not surveyed — it says "this kind of place, this much," not "this parcel, by this date" — and its rate as front-loaded on purpose, so the change is visible well before the multi-century commitment it's built on has fully landed.

BORDER STABILITY

Turn on POWER or CONFLICT and the real political-border lines recolor by the same conflict-risk composite used everywhere else in the interface. A red, pulsing border means the ground on at least one side of it is under elevated modelled pressure. This layer never moves the line — it's the honest half of the border picture, the part that's a direct read of a number the rest of the map already shows.

SPECULATIVE TERRITORY

The rose-colored dashed line, also under CONFLICT, is the dishonest-if-unlabeled half — so it's labeled everywhere it appears. It is not derived from any dataset: it's what happens when you push the model's own power and conflict composites through a rule ("the stronger, less internally contested side's border creeps toward the weaker one") and let it draw. Near term this is barely visible by design — a border cannot plausibly have moved by next year — and it grows into the scenario and speculative era the way everything else on this map does. As it strengthens, today's real border fades in step, so you're never looking at two borders at once: the line you see is either what's real today or what the model is projecting instead, not both stacked together. Read the rose line as "here is a shape conflict pressure could take," never as "here is the new border."

WHAT IT WILL NEVER DO

Name a war. State a date for one. Present the rose speculative line as a claim about an actual future border. Present a modeled output as measured fact. Every conflict signal is framed as elevated probability under current conditions — never as an event.
